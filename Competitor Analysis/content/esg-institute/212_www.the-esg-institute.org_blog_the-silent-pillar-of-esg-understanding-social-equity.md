# The Silent Pillar of ESG - Understanding Social Equity
Oct 6
Written By [Jaime Amoedo](https://www.the-esg-institute.org/blog?author=6495d39d82ae3248fe49c1ca)
![](https://images.squarespace-cdn.com/content/v1/642e883205cdec341bcebf38/2fa1d3e3-50ab-4451-ba12-2674822bd89d/Social+The+ESG+Institute.png)
Establishing a rich dialogue with a diverse set of stakeholders is fundamental to grasping and addressing social equity challenges within the business milieu. This should be an ongoing engagement, forming the foundation of an organization's social equity strategy. Through engagement, corporations can acquire valuable insights into the expectations and aspirations of the communities and individuals they impact.
Stakeholder engagement is a multi-faceted endeavor, encompassing a wide array of groups and individuals, including employees, customers, suppliers, community members, advocacy groups, and even competitors. Each has a unique perspective and vested interest in a company's social equity performance.
A 2020 study by **McKinsey** revealed that companies with greater gender diversity on their executive teams were 25% more likely to experience above-average profitability. Moreover, a report by **Boston Consulting Group** found that companies with more diverse management teams have 19% higher revenues due to innovation. Furthermore, a **Nielsen** survey disclosed that 66% of global consumers are willing to pay more for products from companies committed to positive social and environmental impact, indicating a growing consumer consciousness towards socially responsible businesses.
In this article, I share examples of social equity initiatives from a wide range of sectors, highlighting the efforts of companies such as Intel, Starbucks, Levi Strauss, Unilever, Procter & Gamble, Ben & Jerry's, and Salesforce.
For instance, **Starbucks** ’ response to a racial bias incident in 2018 exemplifies constructive stakeholder engagement. Following the wrongful arrest of two Black men in one of its Philadelphia stores, Starbucks engaged in a comprehensive stakeholder engagement process. This process involved discussions with employees, community leaders, and social justice advocates to understand the incident's broader societal implications and the specific biases that led to it. The insights gleaned from these engagements informed a company-wide racial bias training program, a step towards cultivating a more inclusive and equitable environment for both employees and customers.
Similarly, the apparel brand **Levi Strauss & Co.** has a long history of engaging with stakeholders to address social equity concerns. Through regular consultations with labor unions, advocacy groups, and local communities, the brand has been able to understand and address issues such as fair labor practices and community impact. These engagements have not only informed Levi Strauss & Co.’s social equity initiatives but have also helped build trust and foster collaborative relationships with stakeholders.
Moreover, I consider that proactive engagement in understanding and anticipating social equity challenges is a hallmark of forward-thinking companies. **Unilever** , for instance, through its Sustainable Living Plan, regularly engages with stakeholders to gauge their concerns and aspirations concerning social, environmental, and economic issues. These engagements inform Unilever’s strategic initiatives aimed at enhancing social equity and broader sustainability.
The essence of stakeholder engagement lies in fostering mutual understanding and a collaborative approach towards social equity. Through open dialogue, active listening, and adaptability, companies can significantly bolster their social equity performance, leading to stronger relationships, enhanced brand reputation, and ultimately, a more sustainable and inclusive business model.
**Historical Context**
Embarking on a journey towards social equity within ESG assessments necessitates a fundamental step: conducting a baseline assessment. This assessment serves as a reflection of the existing state of social equity within an organization, laying down a factual basis for crafting and implementing improvement strategies.
A meticulous collection and analysis of data pertaining to various facets of social equity such as diversity and inclusion, wage equality, fair treatment, and community engagement are indispensable for a baseline assessment. Utilizing established frameworks like the **Global Reporting Initiative (GRI)** or the **Sustainability Accounting Standards Board (SASB)** can offer a structured approach.
For instance, in its quest for enhancing diversity and inclusion, Intel conducted a comprehensive assessment to understand the representation of women and underrepresented minorities within its workforce. The findings spurred a company-wide initiative aimed at achieving full representation by 2020, a goal it successfully met.
Similarly, **Procter & Gamble (P&G)** undertook a rigorous assessment of its gender equality practices. The insights garnered led to a series of initiatives promoting gender equality both internally and within its broader value chain. By setting a clear benchmark through its initial assessment, P&G could measure progress and adapt its strategies to better meet its social equity goals.
![](https://images.squarespace-cdn.com/content/v1/642e883205cdec341bcebf38/59664647-6ce8-46aa-a3ac-0d635a12845e/Unilever+Sustainable+Living+Plan.png)
Moreover, a baseline assessment is not a siloed effort but often necessitates engagement with various stakeholders for a holistic understanding of social equity performance. For instance, **Salesforce** engaged its employees and external experts in its assessment of pay equity, leading to salary adjustments to rectify identified disparities.
The insights acquired from a baseline assessment are instrumental in setting a clear trajectory for enhancing social equity. They enable companies to set realistic and impactful targets, allocate resources wisely, and devise informed strategies. Furthermore, openly sharing the findings with stakeholders can build trust and exhibit a genuine commitment to advancing social equity.
The rapidly evolving ESG landscape mandates a robust baseline assessment of social equity for businesses dedicated to nurturing a more equitable and inclusive corporate environment. Through meticulously conducted assessments, companies not only enhance their social equity performance but also build stronger relationships with stakeholders, fortify their brand reputation, and contribute to a more equitable and sustainable business ecosystem.
**Importance of Social Equity**
Over recent years, the importance of social equity within the business arena has magnified, transcending beyond traditional corporate social responsibility bounds to become a core determinant of a company’s long-term success and sustainability. At the heart of social equity lies the ethos of fairness, inclusivity, and equal opportunity, extending beyond organizational boundaries to the wider community and society.
A quintessential aspect of social equity is its potential to foster an inclusive workplace culture. When individuals from diverse backgrounds feel valued, respected, and included, it invariably leads to enhanced morale and productivity. Salesforce underscored this notion through a comprehensive review of its pay structure to rectify any gender and racial pay disparities, reflecting not just a commitment to social equity but a strategic endeavor to nurture an inclusive work culture conducive to employee satisfaction and retention.
Beyond the organizational ambit, social equity significantly impacts a company's brand reputation and customer loyalty. In an era where consumers align their purchasing decisions with their values, companies championing social equity are likely to engender stronger customer loyalty. **Ben & Jerry's**, with its longstanding commitment to social justice causes, exemplifies this principle, cultivating a fiercely loyal customer base while resonating with a broader audience, enhancing the brand’s reputation and market appeal.
Moreover, the legal and regulatory landscape is gradually evolving to integrate social equity considerations. For instance, the European Union's Non-Financial Reporting Directive mandates companies to disclose social and employee-related practices, indicating a shift towards more transparent and accountable business practices. Compliance with such frameworks not only mitigates legal risks but also positions companies favorably in the eyes of investors and stakeholders, who are progressively factoring in social equity in their evaluation of a company’s ESG performance.
The trajectory of social equity’s integration within the business realm reflects a broader societal acknowledgment of its intrinsic value. Companies like **Unilever** and **Patagonia** underscore the burgeoning recognition of social equity as a pivotal aspect of a company’s ESG profile. As this narrative continues to gain momentum, the impetus for businesses to embrace and advance social equity is poised to intensify, heralding a new epoch where social equity is not an ancillary consideration, but a fundamental tenet of business sustainability and success.

**Current Status of Social Equity in ESG Assessment**
As the ESG landscape evolves, the incorporation of social equity metrics is gaining traction, albeit at a pace that highlights the nascent stage of this integration. The present status of social equity within ESG assessments presents a tableau of earnest endeavors interspersed with challenges.
Established frameworks like the **Sustainability Accounting Standards Board** (SASB) and the **Global Reporting Initiative** (GRI) have made strides in incorporating metrics evaluating social equity. For instance, Intel’s Corporate Responsibility Report, a yearly disclosure, outlines the company’s efforts in enhancing diversity and inclusion, setting a precedent for other tech companies.
However, the journey towards robust integration of social equity in ESG assessments encounters notable hurdles, such as disparity in standards and the lack of universal metrics, hindering accurate comparison and evaluation of companies' social equity performance. According to **McKinsey** , firms with high gender diversity are 21% more likely to outperform their counterparts, and those with high ethnic diversity are 33% more likely to have industry-leading profitability, showcasing the economic incentive behind integrating social equity metrics.
Moreover, the discourse around social equity within the ESG realm is often overshadowed by the more quantifiable environmental and governance metrics, belying the intrinsic interconnectedness of the E, S, and G components, and the critical role social equity plays in a company’s overall sustainability and ethical standing. A 2021 report by **Deloitte** highlighted that 92% of companies surveyed viewed diversity, equity, and inclusion as a priority, but only 6% believed they had succeeded in achieving an inclusive culture, indicating the gap between intentions and actual integration of social equity metrics.
Yet, amidst these challenges, there's a burgeoning recognition of social equity's importance within the ESG framework. Investors, regulators, and consumers are increasingly holding companies accountable for their social equity practices. This growing awareness is nudging companies to enhance their social equity assessments and disclosures. In fact, a survey by **EY** revealed that 74% of investors are interested in ESG information, with social factors being a significant part of the evaluation.
The narrative of social equity within ESG assessments is a dynamic one, with its contours being shaped by evolving societal expectations, regulatory developments, and the actions of pioneering companies. As more companies like Salesforce and Unilever take the helm in integrating social equity within their ESG assessments, the benchmark for social equity performance is set to rise, beckoning other companies to follow suit.
The unfolding narrative paints a picture of optimism tempered with the reality of the challenges ahead. Yet, the direction is clear: the integration of social equity within ESG assessments is not an end but a means towards fostering a more equitable, inclusive, and sustainable business ecosystem.

**Emerging Trends**
The contemporary business milieu is witnessing a discernible shift towards a more pronounced acknowledgment of social equity as a cardinal component of ESG assessments. This shift is symptomatic of a deeper societal transformation that views businesses not merely as economic entities but as agents of positive social change.
One of the salient trends is the proliferation of new frameworks and metrics designed to measure and report on social equity aspects. The Human Capital Management Coalition, for instance, is at the forefront of advocating for enhanced disclosure around workforce metrics, recognizing the quintessence of an engaged and diverse workforce in driving business success.
Alongside the evolution of assessment frameworks, a palpable rise in awareness and advocacy for social equity in ESG is discernible. Investor coalitions like "Racial Justice Investing" are pushing the envelope by calling for racial equity audits, aiming to hold companies accountable for their commitments to social equity. This burgeoning advocacy is igniting a broader discourse on social equity, nudging companies to re-evaluate and bolster their social equity practices.
Furthermore, the advent of social equity-focused investment funds exemplifies the growing investor interest in social equity. Funds such as the "**NAACP Minority Empowerment ETF** " embody this trend, offering investors an avenue to channel their capital towards companies with strong social equity practices.
Moreover, industry collaborations aimed at advancing social equity are gaining traction. Companies are increasingly joining forces with their peers, non-profits, and advocacy groups to address systemic social equity challenges. For instance, the "**Time to Vote** " initiative, which saw a coalition of companies committing to ensure their employees have the time to vote, underscores the potential of collaborative action in advancing social equity.
Added to these trends is the 2023 focus on Ethics and Integrity, emphasizing not merely stating ESG ambitions but translating those ambitions into actionable endeavors. This reflects a growing discernment among stakeholders evaluating not just what businesses say, but what they do in terms of social equity.
The transition towards integrating sustainability into mainstream business, driven by rising ESG regulations and disclosure requirements, is another notable trend. The sustainable debt market's anticipated pivotal role in advancing sustainability objectives also highlights the financial sector's role in promoting social equity. Lastly, the renewed global commitment to act on biodiversity, amidst the ongoing energy crisis, is expected to broaden corporate sustainability agendas to encompass social equity concerns, possibly leading to more holistic and inclusive ESG assessments.
The confluence of these trends paints an optimistic picture of the trajectory of social equity within ESG assessments. As new frameworks emerge, advocacy intensifies, and collaborative efforts burgeon, the canvas of social equity within ESG is set to become richer and more nuanced. The road ahead, albeit laden with challenges, is paved with a collective resolve to foster a more equitable, inclusive, and sustainable business ecosystem, heralding a promising era where social equity is ingrained in the very fabric of business sustainability and ethics.

**Future Implications**
The trajectory of social equity's incorporation within ESG assessments is reflective of a broader narrative, heralding a more accountable and inclusive business future. The unfolding narrative is intertwined with various implications that are set to redefine the business landscape.
One of the significant implications is the potential shift in regulatory compliance. Regulatory bodies worldwide are now seen augmenting existing compliance frameworks to encompass social equity metrics, akin to the European Union's Non-Financial Reporting Directive. This directive mandates companies to disclose social and employee-related practices, marking a step towards more transparent and accountable business practices.
Moreover, investor expectations are changing. Investors are progressively factoring in social equity when evaluating a company's ESG performance. The "**Say on Pay** " movement, where shareholders vote on executive compensation, reflects a growing investor interest in equitable pay practices. This movement has seen companies like **Citigroup Inc.** being urged by investors to disclose gender pay gaps, which is indicative of a broader trend where investors are seeking more disclosure on social equity issues.
Furthermore, the long-term benefits for businesses investing in social equity are becoming apparent. Companies like **Unilever** have reaped the benefits of investing in social equity through initiatives like the **Sustainable Living Plan**. This plan encompasses various social equity initiatives that have not only bolstered Unilever’s brand image but have also driven sustainable growth. Similarly, Patagonia’s commitment to social equity, manifested through fair labor practices and environmental advocacy, has enhanced its brand reputation, fostering a loyal customer base and attracting like-minded employees.
The increasing integration of social equity within ESG assessment frameworks is a testimony to a growing recognition of its intrinsic value. It’s a journey of transformation, where companies, investors, and regulators are adapting to an evolving paradigm. As social equity metrics become more refined and the narrative around social equity continues to gain traction, the business community is poised to witness a shift towards a more equitable and sustainable business ecosystem.
As companies like **Salesforce** , **Intel** , and many others continue to lead the charge in integrating social equity within their ESG assessments, the narrative around social equity and its indispensable role within ESG assessments is poised to become a mainstay in the global business discourse. This shift is not just a fleeting trend but a reflection of evolving societal values and a collective endeavor towards fostering a more equitable, inclusive, and sustainable business ecosystem.

**Strategies for Incorporating Social Equity in ESG Assessment**
Incorporating social equity within the ESG assessment framework is a nuanced endeavor that necessitates a strategic approach. As businesses seek to enhance their ESG profiles, adopting robust strategies to assess and improve social equity within their operations and broader stakeholder engagements is paramount.
One of the key steps towards this endeavor is identifying and engaging stakeholders. Engaging with a diverse group of stakeholders helps in understanding their concerns and expectations regarding social equity. **Starbucks** exemplified this approach in response to a racial bias incident in 2018, engaging in discussions with employees, community leaders, and social justice advocates to understand the broader societal implications and the specific biases that led to the incident. The insights gleaned from these engagements informed a company-wide racial bias training program.
Conducting a baseline assessment using established frameworks like GRI or SASB is another pivotal step. A baseline assessment lays down a factual basis upon which improvement strategies can be crafted and implemented. **Intel** , for instance, conducted a comprehensive assessment to understand the representation of women and underrepresented minorities within its workforce, which spurred a company-wide initiative aimed at achieving full representation in its workforce by 2020.
Setting clear, measurable social equity goals aligned with broader business objectives and stakeholder expectations is also crucial. Setting targets provides a clear direction and helps in monitoring progress. Companies like **Procter & Gamble** have set clear benchmarks through initial assessments, which have helped in measuring progress and adapting strategies to better meet social equity goals.
Implementing initiatives aimed at enhancing social equity within the organization and in interactions with external stakeholders is another pivotal strategy. Developing and implementing such initiatives require a collaborative approach, engaging various stakeholders to ensure the initiatives are well-rounded and effective.
Moreover, monitoring progress against set targets and transparently reporting findings to stakeholders is fundamental for maintaining accountability and fostering a culture of continuous improvement. Salesforce's initiative of conducting a regular assessment of pay equity and transparently sharing the findings is a testament to this strategy.
Additionally, utilizing tools and resources like the B Impact Assessment and engaging with consultancies specializing in ESG and social equity can provide invaluable insights and support in enhancing social equity performance. Engaging in industry collaborations and participating in initiatives focused on advancing social equity are also vital strategies.

As more companies integrate social equity within their ESG assessments, learning from pioneering companies and adapting to emerging trends will be key. The journey towards integrating social equity within ESG assessments is a continuous process, one that requires a concerted effort from businesses, investors, regulators, and society at large. The examples of companies like **Patagonia** , **Unilever** , and others leading the charge in this domain underscore the criticality of advancing social equity within the ESG framework, beckoning other companies to follow suit in fostering a more inclusive and equitable business landscape.
**Conclusion**
The exploration of social equity within the ESG assessment framework unveils a dynamic and evolving narrative. It reflects a broader societal shift towards recognizing and addressing social equity concerns within the business domain. This shift is not merely a reactive endeavor, but a proactive pursuit towards fostering a more equitable and sustainable business landscape.
The real-world examples of companies like **Salesforce** , **Unilever** , and **Starbucks** underline the tangible steps businesses are taking to embed social equity within their ESG assessments and broader operational ethos. These companies are not only setting benchmarks in social equity performance but are also exemplifying the potential positive impacts of such endeavors. The benefits span enhanced brand reputation, stronger stakeholder relationships, improved legal compliance, and a conducive environment for sustainable growth.
Moreover, the increasing integration of social equity metrics within established ESG assessment frameworks like GRI and SASB is indicative of a growing recognition of social equity’s significance. This integration is a crucial step towards fostering a more standardized and holistic approach to assessing and enhancing social equity within the business realm.
Furthermore, the emerging trends and future implications discussed in the preceding sections illuminate the ongoing evolution in the social equity discourse within ESG assessments. They highlight the growing emphasis on ethics, transparency, and accountability, and how these elements are gradually becoming intertwined with a company’s ESG performance.
In conclusion, the journey towards fully integrating social equity within ESG assessments is a complex yet rewarding endeavor. It beckons a collective effort from businesses, investors, regulators, and society at large. As more companies embark on this journey, sharing best practices, learning from challenges, and adapting to evolving societal expectations will be pivotal. The narrative around social equity within ESG assessments is not only reshaping business practices but is also contributing to a broader societal shift towards equity, inclusivity, and sustainability. This narrative is poised to continue evolving, driven by a shared aspiration to foster a more equitable and sustainable global business ecosystem.
