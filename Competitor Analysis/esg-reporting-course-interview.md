# ESG Reporting Course - Interview Notes

## Round 1: Foundation

### Audience & Outcome
- **Primary learner:** Someone brand new to the domain - either a fresher or an industry professional who has never done ESG reporting before
- They don't know: how to start, where to start, what the standards are, how the process works, whether they need a consultant, what the entire process looks like
- Also targets: first-time consultants who need to know what to ask and what the client won't volunteer
- **After completing the course:** Learner should understand the entire ESG reporting process end-to-end and feel comfortable about how the process is going to look. They should be able to work on it confidently.

### The "Practical" Philosophy
- This is about teaching the **workflow** - how ESG reporting actually gets done in practice
- How you interact with people, how you think about writing a report
- NOT a clause-by-clause walkthrough of frameworks/standards (those will be separate courses)
- Focus on the messy reality, not the textbook version

### Prajjwal's Background
- **Role:** Consultant on the advisory side (not in-house)
- **Client types:** All types of organizations
- **Experience:** Full end-to-end ESG reporting engagements

### Top Mistakes Companies Make
1. **No clear agenda for ESG reporting** - They don't know WHY they want an ESG report. This is the single biggest problem.
2. **Data not collected or sorted in one place** - Data is scattered, unorganized, not ready for reporting

## Round 2: The Full Process, Data, People & Timelines

### How an Engagement Typically Starts

**Scenario A - Company starting fresh:**
- They don't know what they want, but they say "we want this and that"
- They may have seen peers do it and want to look at them (peer benchmarking angle)
- Everyone reports roughly the same things; only the design and language changes

**Scenario B - Company with previous reports (so-called "mature"):**
- They have 1-2 past ESG/sustainability reports
- They ask you to refer to those and "innovate" - but they don't really know what innovation means here; they just want some newness

**Common thread:** Even at the start, they're not clear about what they want out of it, what the purpose is, or what to report in it.

### The End-to-End Process (as it actually happens)

**Step 1: Peer Benchmarking**
- Look at what peers are reporting, what standards they reference
- GRI becomes the best base because:
  - Most comprehensive set of standards
  - Majorly interoperable with other standards (regulatory or general KPIs)
  - Most comprehensive set of KPIs
  - Still remains the bare-bones structure regardless of what else companies reference
- Companies may also reference: SASB materiality map, TCFD, IFRS, UNPRI indexing - but these remain indexed/referenced elements; GRI is still the structural backbone

**Step 2: Initial Kickoff Discussion with Client**
- Understand what standard(s) they want to follow
- Align on scope and intent

**Step 3: Share the Data Requirement Sheet**
- A template based on GRI structure
- Ask client to fill data for last 2-3 years (+ current year if available)
- Follows strict GRI format; consulting firms generally already have this Excel template ready
- Provide guidance on how to fill it
- **If the company has past reports + you've done peer benchmarking:** You do NOT share the full GRI suite (it's very comprehensive). Instead, you:
  - Research what peers report
  - Look at what the company has reported before
  - Select only relevant indicators/principles from GRI 200 (Economic), 300 (Environmental), 400 (Social) series
  - Example standards: 305 (Emissions), 304 (Biodiversity), 401 (Employment), etc.
  - Share only the selected subset as the template

**Step 4: The Client Goes Silent**
- Client takes their own time to fill data
- You keep engaging throughout:
  - Have discussions with their various teams
  - Give repeated walkthroughs of the same templates
  - Ask them: "Do you have this data? How can you report it?"
  - Check accuracy of data as it comes in (sanity checks)

**Step 5: Prepare Table of Contents (while data collection is ongoing)**
- Clients ask for this
- Mostly a copy from their past reports or the standard market structure
- **Typical structure:**
  1. About the Report section
  2. Chairman's/CEO's Message
  3. Company Overview section
  4. The three pillars: E, S, and G sections
  5. Risk section
  6. Some business elements
  7. Materiality section
  8. Basic structural elements common across the market

**Step 6: Writing Chapters (as data starts arriving)**
- Start writing various chapters
- ChatGPT is used - "it will sound like an AI robot, but that's okay"
- Writing happens in parallel with ongoing data compilation

**Step 7: Data Refinement**
- Even after data starts coming in, it's not ready
- You may need to call with clients and their departments to:
  - Help them collect the data
  - Refine the data
  - Do sanity checks (they will fill in anything)

**Step 8: The Never-Ending Cycle of Revisions**
- Back-and-forth revisions
- By the end: you forget what you wrote, what the client asked you to write, and the client forgets where you started from

**Step 9: Design**
- Either you do the design or the client does it
- But it's always your job to check it
- Get it designed and delivered

**Step 10: Delivery**
- If you manage to do all of the above, you might just get a thank you

### Timeline
- **Ideal:** 2.5 to 3 months
- **Reality:** As long as the client wants, or as long as teams agree, or until the board approves, or until whoever is asking for it is satisfied
- The delay is almost always on the client side

### Stakeholders Involved

**Primary:** Sustainability team (main point of contact)

**Involved on need basis:**
- HR
- Operations
- Finance
- Health & Safety
- CSR
- IT

### Why Companies Want an ESG Report

**Legitimate reasons:**
1. **Investor angle** - investors asking for it
2. **Communication angle** - stakeholder communication tool
3. **Regulatory** - regulations make it necessary
4. **Keeping up with the Joneses** - peers are doing it
5. **Ratings improvement** - use the report to improve ESG ratings (MSCI, Sustainalytics, etc.)

### How You Help Them Discover the "Why"
- Understand where they're coming from - how they arrived at the decision to write this report
- Follow the intent path religiously
- **Key insight:** If you follow their intent well, you get approvals faster - because the company sees their requirements reflected in the report
- The report content doesn't change drastically across audiences (investor vs. regulator vs. ratings); it's a communication thing - the nuances and elements companies want to highlight differ by audience

### The Data Collection Reality

**What it looks like on the ground:**
- Scattered across departments
- Different formats
- Different software systems
- People hearing about this data need for the first time - not prepared to report it
- People who don't want to share
- **This is the #1 reason engagements take so long**
- The #2 reason is getting reviews/approvals

**How you actually get the data:**
1. Explain the need - why you need it, what it's for
2. If that doesn't work: **the force of hierarchy** - escalate to someone senior who can mandate it

### The Consultant's First Meeting - What Separates Success from Failure

**The single most important thing: TIMELINES**
- Always push back on the client's proposed timeline
- They will always want it done early
- It will always be because of THEM that it takes long
- If you've done your work well but they don't revert on time, they'll blame you
- Setting realistic expectations on timelines from day one is critical

### What Clients Won't Tell You (You Have to Dig)
1. **Why they're really doing it** - the true motivation
2. **How soon they actually need it** - real deadline vs. aspirational deadline

### When an Engagement Gets Difficult

**Root cause: People management.** All engagements can be difficult if you can't manage people and expectations.

**Specific red flags:**
1. **Stakeholders who assume they know more than you** - want things on their timeline, assume things are wrong more often than correct
2. **Team hasn't given enough attention to the work** - client picks up on this early and loses confidence
3. **You don't understand the purpose of the report** - you create something else entirely
4. **Mismatched expectations on report length/style:**
   - Some clients want short
   - Some want long
   - Some want different/unique
   - It's all on you to figure this out

## Round 3: Writing, Content, Materiality, Design

### The Writing Process

**The approach - broad outline first, then questions:**
1. When sharing the ToC with the client, also share a **blueprint** - a broader outline showing sub-components of each chapter
2. This acclimatizes the client (and yourself) to what will be written in each section
3. **Prajjwal's personal writing technique:** Start by writing questions you want to answer in that section. Then write answers to those questions based on what you want to communicate. Then remove the questions - only answers remain. Then edit, connect the paragraphs, and polish.

**Writing the Environmental section specifically:**
- It's about all key environmental elements and the business-environment interplay
- **Two directions:** How the business impacts the environment AND how the environment impacts the business
- **From a business perspective:** Identify the core environmental resource most important to that specific business:
  - For some: energy efficiency
  - For some: waste
  - For some: water
  - Depends on industry and operations
- **Communication needs to be very clear**
- **Data needs to be very precise**
- **Targets are very important** - rating companies and investors look at how you're progressing toward targets

**Practitioner tip - making data relatable:**
- Convert savings from renewable energy, water management, waste management into commonly understandable equivalents
- Examples: "equivalent to X cars taken off the road" or "equivalent to planting X trees"
- Gives the report an interesting, accessible touch

### What Makes a Good ESG Report Chapter

1. **Flow** - It should read in a flow. The chapter should not feel siloed from the rest of the report. It should flow seamlessly both within the chapter AND across the entire report.
2. **Logical internal structure** - Sections within a chapter need to link with each other with a logical flow. You cannot structure in a haphazard manner.
3. **Consistent theme** - Follow the same theme throughout the report. This is a key material point. *(Prajjwal suggests: might need a separate module on "How to decide a theme for the report")*
4. **No repetition** -
   - Do not repeat policies in every chapter
   - Do not repeat last year's report content in this year's chapter
5. **Story-like flow** - One paragraph ends and the next begins from there or takes it forward. It reads more like a story in a professional setup. Think of it as a story you want to tell professionally.
6. **Consistent language** -
   - Some companies want first person, some want third person - follow the client's preference consistently
   - Don't switch between first/third person or active/passive voice within the same paragraph
   - A flavor of mix-and-match across the report is fine, but within paragraphs: stay consistent and grammatically correct

### Narrative vs. Data Balance

- **A fully narrative report:** Even if rich, will feel like it's "just narrative" - not purposeful for the reader
- **A fully data-driven report:** You have no story to tell
- **Ideal balance:** Approximately 50/50 narrative to data/tables/charts
- Don't overdo either part

### Materiality - How It Actually Works

**If the company has already done a materiality assessment:**
- They generally have, and they reuse it for the next 2-3 years
- You refer back to it and write the chapter accordingly

**If they haven't done it:**
- Companies are now offering/referring to **double materiality assessment** even if ESRS/CSRD regulations aren't applicable to them - because it's the new buzzword
- **Double materiality = financial materiality + impact materiality combined:**
  - How the business impacts the environment and the world
  - How changes in the world impact the business financially

**How stakeholder surveys are conducted:**
- Go through various discussions
- Choose stakeholder groups (internal and external)
- Double materiality guidance provides a very detailed work plan for how to conduct it
- *Link to existing Double Materiality course on the platform*

**Does materiality actually influence the report?**
- Yes, in a somewhat manner - it impacts and helps choose topics to focus on
- Companies generally follow the same suit on describing materiality
- **Indian context:** Materiality is not taken very seriously/rigorously in practice

### Specific Sections

**Chairman's/CEO's Message:**
- Written by either the consultant team or the client's internal team
- Definitely gets Chairman's approval
- Content is a mix of:
  - Vision for the company
  - How the company has performed
  - Where they are going
- Heavily influenced by the company's annual report (similar section exists there)
- Needs to have a flavor of business + sustainability together
- Include something about targets and how the company is looking at them

**Hardest chapter to write:**
- **The one where you don't have any data.** Period.

**"About the Report" section (boundary, scope, reporting period):**
- Mostly straightforward
- **Key decision:** Standalone or consolidated boundary?
- Whatever boundary you set here is the SAME boundary you follow across the entire report for data
- If they don't have subsidiary data, don't include subsidiaries - chalk it out in the boundary upfront
- Being very clear with the client on this from the start prevents problems later

### Design

**What makes a well-designed ESG report:**
- **Use the company's existing design agency** - the one that does their annual report, investor relations materials, etc.
- They understand the brand's design language: fonts, colors, representation style
- It looks in sync with the annual report (not jarring or different)

**Most common design mistake:**
- Hiring a **new external designer** who is unfamiliar with the company's brand
- They'll try to create something of their own (which can be creative/good) but the company typically doesn't like it because it doesn't match their established brand language

**Deliverable formats:**
- Clients want **all formats**: print-ready PDF, digital, etc.
- Having a **microsite** is also generally advised and helps

## Round 4: Theme, QA, Advice & Industry Trends

### Setting a Report Theme

**The analogy: A theme is like a character arc, episode focus, or movie sequel.**
- If the story keeps changing every 30 minutes in a movie or every episode, you won't like it (unless it's a collection of different stories)
- Same with the report - you have to be consistent across the theme

**How to derive the theme:**
- Generally derived from where the company's focus is for that year or the next couple of years
- Often influenced by the annual report's direction
- You bring in that business focus and add a sustainability lens - that becomes the theme

**If the client doesn't know what theme they want:**
- Suggest 2-3 themes to them
- Provide a brief introduction/idea behind each one
- Show how the report structure would be organized around it
- That's enough for the client to see, react, and suggest what they want

**How to carry a theme through 100+ pages without it feeling forced:**
- **Select an agnostic/generic theme rather than a hyper-specific one**
- **Example of a bad (too specific) theme:** "AI & Sustainability" - you won't be able to thread that through Social or Governance sections without it feeling forced (using AI everywhere)
- **Good themes are broad enough** to naturally connect to E, S, and G sections without stretching
- *(Note: Prajjwal to provide examples of good vs. not-so-good themes for the course)*

### GRI Content Index
- Yes, you create it
- You create it **at the very end**, once everything is sorted and data is collected
- It's the last thing you do

### Assurance/Verification
- If the company wants to get it done, get it done
- It goes at the end or beginning of the report - wherever the client wants
- *(Not a complex decision from the report writer's perspective - it's the company's choice)*

### BRSR (Business Responsibility & Sustainability Reporting - India)
- Generally done along with the **annual report**, not the sustainability report
- In most contexts, it's a separate exercise
- **Critical rule:** If the boundary is the same in BRSR and the sustainability report, the data MUST match
- You cannot have different data in the two documents
- **A lot of companies make mistakes on this** - data inconsistency between BRSR and sustainability report is a common and serious error

### Final QA Review - The Checklist
1. **Index page matching** - table of contents matches actual page numbers
2. **Data matching** - data is consistent across the report (same number isn't reported differently in two places)
3. **Factual correction** - all facts checked
4. **Spell check**
5. **Spacing** - all spaces are correct, formatting is clean
6. **Full read-through** - read the entire report end-to-end
7. **Team review** - get it reviewed by your own team
8. **Fresh eyes** - have a **new person** read it (someone who hasn't been involved in writing it)

### Advice to Your Past Self - What You Wish Someone Had Told You

**"Just go ahead and write it."**
- Most of the time, you keep waiting for the client/company to give you information, narratives, and data
- But you don't get it. You won't get it easily.
- **You have to dig it out yourself:**
  - Go through their past reports
  - Read their other reports (annual report, etc.)
  - Read their policies
  - Read everything available
  - Write it yourself
- Don't expect them to help you with narratives
- **The workflow is:** You write it yourself, share it with them, they find things to fine-tune and correct. That's how it goes.

### What Makes a Report Stand Out vs. Forgettable
- **Balance between visuals, narrative, and data**
- **Keep it short** - if you make it too long, it's useless. A lot of companies do this.
- The reports that stand out manage the interplay of these three elements well

### How the ESG Reporting Landscape Is Changing (2026)
- **Not changing as dramatically as people think**
- **The big concern: AI slop** - a lot of AI-generated content is flooding reports
- You can use AI to write, but **you MUST edit it yourself**
- **Do not send things without checking** - Big Four and other firms have been penalized for this
- Take care of how AI writes - make it more human, more purposeful
- The key differentiator going forward: **human editing and intentionality over AI-generated bulk**

## Round 5: Social, Governance & Data Sanity

### Social Section - Key Topics

**Two main pillars:**
1. **HR / Human Capital**
   - Diversity (definition varies across geographies - important for global companies)
   - Worker benefits
   - Health & Safety
   - Training data (hard to collect - most companies don't have it)
   - Community engagement - interaction, spend, projects
   - Human rights due diligence
   - Supply chain labor practices
   - Grievance mechanisms
   - Compliance with laws (e.g., POSH Act in India, equivalent laws globally)

2. **Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)**
   - CSR spend, projects, impact

**Hardest Social data to collect:**
- Supply chain related data
- Training related data
- Most companies simply don't have these tracked

**Common mistakes in Social section:**
- Definitions of social standards that aren't part of law keep changing
- Example: The definition of diversity changes across geographies and over time
- Need to be careful when writing for global companies - what counts as "diverse" differs

**Indian vs. global difference:**
- Yes, there's a difference. Social standards and their definitions are not universal.

### Governance Section - Key Topics

- **Governance structure** - a very clear governance structure is where to start. A lot of companies don't focus on this enough.
- Board composition, meeting attendance
- Ethics policies, anti-corruption
- Risk oversight, risk management framework
- Executive compensation
- Whistleblower mechanisms
- **Skill set / board competencies** - increasingly asked for by standards like IFRS
- **Tax governance** - very interesting section; how do they manage tax?
- **IT governance and data governance** - a lot of data can come from here; how they manage IT security, data privacy, etc.

**Ease of writing:**
- Governance is the **easiest** section because:
  - It's very well sorted
  - Most of it comes from the annual report and existing policies
  - It's already very well written in other corporate documents
  - You can draw heavily from what exists

### Data Sanity - The Full Picture

**Classic errors to check for:**

1. **Extremes: 0% and 100%**
   - If you say something is 0, it MUST be definitively 0 with backup proof
   - If data is simply not available, that does NOT mean it's 0
   - **Key phrase: "The absence of evidence is not the evidence of absence"**
   - Similarly, you cannot claim 100% unless you can prove it is 100%

2. **Internal consistency**
   - Same metric must not show different numbers in different places within the report
   - Same metric must not differ between the sustainability report and other reports (BRSR, annual report)

3. **Year-on-year changes**
   - Significant swings need explanation
   - Check if methodology changes in calculations caused the shift

4. **Data gaps**
   - Identify what's missing and flag it - don't paper over gaps

5. **Decimal places**
   - Don't use more than two decimal places (generally)

6. **Currency changes**
   - If reporting across years or geographies, currency conversions must be consistent and noted

7. **GHG inventory definitions and breakups**
   - The definitions and category breakups of the GHG inventory must be consistent and clear

8. **Numbers that don't add up**
   - Totals must match the sum of their parts. Always.

**How to verify data accuracy:**
- You generally can't verify against original source unless they share it with you
- **If assurance is done**, it significantly increases reliance on data quality
- **Best practice:** Get the final data only AFTER assurance is complete
- Otherwise, you'll be making endless changes as assurance findings come in

**Worst data quality disaster:**
- **Data coming in 10 different times** and forgetting which version is which
- Very common across companies
- **Never get into that situation** - version control on data is critical

## Round 6: Stakeholder Archetypes & Review Management

### The Two Stakeholder Types to Watch Out For

**DRONGON (Wrong-ons):**
- People who do NOT understand the work or what is being done
- But they keep coming back with an assumption that they understand everything
- They want things "improved" in a certain way
- Their responses are not rooted in anything substantive
- Feedback is generic OR extremely minuscule ("this comma is missed", "this spelling is wrong")
- Nothing technical or communication-oriented
- They want everything reviewed again and again and again
- They want to get on a call for everything
- Key trait: high noise, low signal

**BOGON (Bog-ons):**
- People who will NOT give you anything during the process
- Then they put everything on YOU later when the work is not done
- They'll either claim they gave you everything (they didn't) or deny they were ever asked
- **Defense:** Keep a trail of emails and messages showing you repeatedly asked for data
- Key trait: absent during work, present during blame

### Review Process Management
- Lots of reviews will happen - that's normal
- But you must not go back and forth too many times on data and content
- Be very clear with messages and how the review process is structured
- This is where most of the time in an engagement goes
- Set expectations on review rounds upfront
