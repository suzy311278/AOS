The Glasgow Rulebook

12:45 / 24:00

The Final Piece of the Puzzle

After years of negotiation, the rules governing Article 6 were finally agreed at COP26 in Glasgow in November 2021. Known as the "Glasgow Rulebook," these decisions transformed Article 6 from an aspirational framework into an operational system with clear procedures, safeguards, and institutional architecture. The rulebook addressed the most contentious issues that had stalled progress since Paris, including how to avoid double counting of emission reductions and how to ensure environmental integrity across diverse national contexts.

lightbulb
Key Takeaway

The Glasgow Rulebook ensures that carbon credits cannot be counted twice by requiring "corresponding adjustments" to national inventories. When a country sells emission reductions abroad, it must add those emissions back to its own ledger, maintaining the overall integrity of the Paris Agreement.

The significance of the Glasgow outcome cannot be overstated. For six years, Parties had been unable to resolve fundamental questions about market mechanisms under the Paris Agreement. The failure to reach consensus at COP25 in Madrid in 2019 had raised serious concerns about whether international carbon trading could function under the new climate regime. Glasgow changed that calculus entirely, establishing a comprehensive set of rules that enabled real transactions to begin.

The Three Pillars of Implementation

The Glasgow Rulebook operationalizes three distinct cooperative approaches, each designed to serve different types of climate cooperation between countries:

check_circle
Article 6.2 (Bilateral Cooperation)

Enables direct country-to-country trading of Internationally Transferred Mitigation Outcomes (ITMOs), with corresponding adjustments to both nations' NDC accounting.

check_circle
Article 6.4 (Crediting Mechanism)

Creates a centralized, UN-supervised crediting mechanism (successor to the CDM) that issues credits from approved mitigation activities worldwide.

check_circle
Article 6.8 (Non-Market Approaches)

Facilitates cooperative action that does not involve trading of credits, such as technology transfer, capacity building, and results-based climate finance.

psychology
Analogy

Think of the Glasgow Rulebook as the software that runs an international banking system. Article 6.2 is like a peer-to-peer payment app (direct transfers between users), Article 6.4 is like a centralized bank (with standardized accounts and oversight), and Article 6.8 is like a grant program (non-commercial cooperation). All three rely on the same ledger to prevent anyone from spending the same dollar twice.

One of the most consequential features of the Glasgow Rulebook is the "Share of Proceeds" provision. Under Article 6.4, a mandatory 5% of credits issued must be cancelled to deliver overall mitigation beyond what would have occurred otherwise. Additionally, 2% of credits are directed to the Adaptation Fund, generating revenue for developing countries to adapt to climate impacts. This dual-purpose mechanism ensures that carbon markets not only offset emissions but actively contribute to global climate ambition.

science
Example

Switzerland and Ghana signed one of the first Article 6.2 agreements in 2020. Under this deal, Switzerland finances electric bus fleets in Ghana's capital, Accra, replacing diesel-powered public transit. The emission reductions generated are split: Ghana applies a corresponding adjustment to its NDC, transferring the credited tonnes to Switzerland, which counts them toward its own climate target. Both countries benefit, but the atmospheric outcome is preserved because the reductions are only counted once.
