## Why Integration Matters

The ISSB designed IFRS S2 so that the four pillars form a coherent narrative. An investor reading a complete IFRS S2 disclosure should be able to trace a clear path:

1. **Governance** identifies *who* is responsible for climate risk oversight
2. **Strategy** shows *what* climate risks and opportunities the entity faces and how it has responded
3. **Risk Management** describes *how* the entity identifies and manages those risks
4. **Metrics and Targets** demonstrates *how well* the entity is performing against its plans

If governance disclosures describe a Board Sustainability Committee that "oversees the climate transition plan," the strategy disclosures must describe that plan. If the plan includes a 2030 emissions reduction target, the Metrics and Targets section must show progress against it. If governance mentions executive remuneration is linked to a climate metric, paragraph 29(g) must disclose what that metric is.

<AnalogyBox>
Think of the four pillars as different chapters of the same story. The governance chapter introduces the characters. The strategy chapter describes the plot. Risk management describes the obstacles. Metrics and targets shows whether the characters are overcoming those obstacles. If the chapters contradict each other or describe different stories, the narrative breaks down, and investors notice.
</AnalogyBox>

## The "No Unnecessary Duplication" Principle

Paragraph 7 provides an important practical efficiency: entities that manage climate risks on an *integrated* basis with other sustainability topics can provide **integrated governance disclosures** rather than repeating the same information for each topic separately.

For example, if a company's Board Sustainability Committee oversees all ESG topics (climate, biodiversity, social), the company need not describe the committee three times (once under IFRS S2, once under any biodiversity disclosure, once under social topics). A single, integrated description that clearly indicates which elements apply to climate satisfies IFRS S2.

This principle was reinforced in the Basis for Conclusions (BC31 to BC32): the ISSB closely aligned IFRS S2 governance requirements with IFRS S1's general requirements to ensure consistency and avoid duplication across sustainability topics.

## Remuneration Linked to Sustainability Performance

One of the strongest signals of genuine climate governance is whether executive compensation is tied to sustainability outcomes. Paragraph 6(a)(v) requires disclosure of how the governance body oversees target-setting and whether related performance metrics are used in remuneration policies.

In practice, companies disclose remuneration linkages with varying levels of detail. The most informative disclosures explain the approval process, the incentive structure, the specific metrics used, and the weighting applied.

<ExampleBox>
**Example (CEO compensation framework):**

The annual remuneration of board members and its committees is approved by shareholders at the Annual General Meeting. The compensation structure is aligned with the company's climate and broader sustainability-related targets by incorporating sustainability-related considerations into relevant business areas.

The company established a long-term incentive plan in 2022 that conditionally entitles members of the Board, its committees, and key management personnel to shares. Vesting is subject to the company's financial and non-financial (sustainability-related) performance against metrics and targets established by the Board, as well as a three-year service condition.

The CEO's scorecard for annual variable compensation includes **15% relating to sustainability matters**, split equally between:
- **Net-zero progress** (7.5%): measured against the 2030 emissions reduction target
- **Other sustainability-related metrics** (7.5%): covering health and safety, water efficiency, and gender pay gap reduction

The specific sustainability performance metrics and targets are described in further detail in the Metrics and Targets section of this course (Lesson 7.4).
</ExampleBox>

<HighlightBox>
The standards also require disclosures on remuneration under the Metrics and Targets pillar (paragraph 29(g)). Companies should consider whether consolidating remuneration information into one section of the report could improve clarity and avoid duplication. Cross-referencing between governance and metrics sections is common practice.
</HighlightBox>

## How Governance Connects to Strategy

Governance disclosures should make clear that the identified governance bodies are actually engaged with the strategic decisions described in the Strategy pillar. Signs of meaningful connectivity include:

- The governance body is involved in approving or reviewing the transition plan (Para 14)
- The governance body oversees climate-related target-setting (Para 6(a)(v)) and those targets appear in the Metrics and Targets section (Para 33 to 37)
- Major transactions described in the strategy section (acquisitions, divestments) are subject to the climate assessment process described in governance

## How Governance Connects to Risk Management

Risk management disclosures describe the processes used to identify and assess climate risks. Governance disclosures should show that the governance body receives the outputs of those processes:

- How often the board is informed about climate risks (Para 6(a)(iii)) should align with the monitoring processes described in Para 25(a)(v)
- If the risk management process uses scenario analysis (Para 25(a)(ii)), the governance body should be briefed on scenario outcomes

## How Governance Connects to Metrics and Targets

The explicit connection in Para 6(a)(v), where the governance body oversees target-setting and monitors progress, means investors can check whether the governance body's remuneration linkages are aligned with the targets disclosed in Para 33 to 37.

<ExampleBox>
**Example of disconnected governance (a common quality issue):**

A company's governance disclosure states: "The Board Sustainability Committee oversees our net-zero commitment and reviews progress annually."

But the strategy section does not mention a net-zero commitment. The metrics section does not include GHG emissions targets. The risk management section does not mention climate risk monitoring.

This creates a credibility gap. The governance disclosure claims oversight of something that does not appear anywhere else in the disclosure. An investor would rightly question whether the governance is substantive.

**A connected disclosure** would: describe the net-zero commitment in strategy (Para 14), set out specific interim targets in Metrics (Para 33), report current emissions against those targets (Para 29(a)), and reference the committee's quarterly review of progress.
</ExampleBox>

## The Journey to Full Integration

For many companies, integrating sustainability into governance is not a single event but a multi-year journey. It is common, and acceptable under the standard, to disclose where the company currently stands on this journey and what steps are planned.

A typical integration timeline might look like this:

<ResponsiveTable>
<table class="mb-6">
  <tr><th>Year</th><th>Milestone</th><th>What Changed</th></tr>
  <tr><td>FY2022</td><td>Integrated identification and assessment</td><td>First year that climate risk management capabilities were introduced into business activities and processes, aligned with TCFD recommendations.</td></tr>
  <tr><td>FY2024</td><td>Broadened the focus beyond climate</td><td>Expanded the sustainability approach to encompass not only climate-related risks but also emerging priorities such as nature and human rights.</td></tr>
  <tr><td>FY2025</td><td>Operational risk integrated</td><td>Updated the operational risk policy to assess SROs. Introduced sustainability regulatory horizon scanning into the compliance risk policy.</td></tr>
  <tr><td>FY2026</td><td>Monitoring update</td><td>Additional measures to enhance SROs monitoring will be developed.</td></tr>
  <tr><td>FY2027</td><td>Full integration</td><td>Sustainability-related risks will be quantified and fully integrated into the Enterprise Risk Management Framework (ERMF). SROs will be considered in manufacturing processes. Complete systemic application of sustainability analytics will holistically inform the overall risk assessment.</td></tr>
</table>
</ResponsiveTable>

This type of disclosure is particularly useful for companies that are less advanced in embedding sustainability into their broader risk management. It demonstrates a credible path toward full integration and allows users to track progress year over year.

<HighlightBox>
Disclosing the integration journey builds investor confidence even when the company has not yet achieved full integration. Transparency about where you are and where you are heading is more valuable than overstating current capabilities.
</HighlightBox>
