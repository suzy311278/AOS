## The Two Categories of Climate Risk

IFRS S2 paragraph 3 establishes two categories of climate-related risk:

1. **Climate-related physical risks:** Risks from the physical effects of climate change
2. **Climate-related transition risks:** Risks from the process of transitioning to a lower-carbon economy

These categories are not mutually exclusive. A single event (for example, a new carbon tax) is a transition risk. But the physical disruption that motivates the policy (for example, rising temperatures) is a physical risk. Companies often face both simultaneously.

## Physical Risks: Acute and Chronic

IFRS S2 Appendix A distinguishes two types of physical risk:

**Acute physical risks** are event-driven, meaning extreme weather events that occur suddenly:

- Tropical storms and hurricanes
- Floods (riverine, coastal, and flash)
- Heatwaves
- Wildfires
- Droughts
- Extreme precipitation events

**Chronic physical risks** arise from longer-term shifts in climate patterns:

- Rising mean temperatures
- Sea level rise
- Changes in precipitation patterns and frequency
- Biodiversity loss and ecosystem degradation
- Changes in soil productivity and water availability

## Transition Risks: Five Subcategories

IFRS S2 Appendix A defines transition risks as risks arising from the transition to a lower-carbon economy, across five subcategories:

**Policy risks** (changes in government regulations and policy):
- Carbon pricing mechanisms (emissions trading schemes, carbon taxes)
- Mandatory energy efficiency standards
- Prohibition of certain technologies or fuels

**Legal risks** (litigation and regulatory enforcement arising from climate-related obligations):
- Climate liability lawsuits against high-emission companies
- Liability for failure to adapt or disclose climate risks adequately
- Regulatory enforcement actions for non-compliance with emissions standards

**Technological risks** (disruption from new low-carbon technologies):
- Rapid cost reduction in renewable energy stranding fossil fuel assets
- Electrification of transport affecting internal combustion engine manufacturers
- Carbon capture and storage costs affecting industrial competitiveness

**Market risks** (changing supply and demand patterns):
- Shifting consumer preferences toward low-carbon products
- Increasing raw material costs from carbon pricing on supply chains
- Changing investor preferences affecting access to capital

**Reputational risks** (changing perceptions of an entity's role in climate change):
- Consumer boycotts of high-emission companies
- "Greenwashing" accusations
- Difficulty attracting talent or partners

<AnalogyBox>
The difference between physical and transition risks is like the difference between weather risk and regulatory risk in a traditional industry analysis. A fishery faces both: a bad storm season (physical risk) can destroy boats and reduce catches; a new fishing quota regulation (transition risk equivalent) can limit operations regardless of weather. In climate, both are real and often interconnected. Governments sometimes introduce regulations precisely because physical risks are worsening.
</AnalogyBox>

## Climate-Related Opportunities

IFRS S2 also requires disclosure of **climate-related opportunities**, the potential positive effects from climate change or the transition. These can include:

- **Resource efficiency:** Reducing operating costs through energy efficiency
- **New energy sources:** Revenues from renewable energy development
- **Products and services:** New markets for low-carbon or climate-resilient products
- **New markets:** Access to green finance, carbon credits, or sustainability-linked markets
- **Resilience:** Competitive advantage from more resilient supply chains and operations

## Time Horizons

Every risk and opportunity must be assigned a time horizon. IFRS S2 paragraph 10(d) requires the entity to define what short, medium, and long term mean for its circumstances, linked to its planning horizons. There is no universal definition, but common approaches include:

## Presenting an Overview of SROs in Practice

In practice, companies present their identified risks and opportunities in a structured tabular format that consolidates the risk description, time horizon, and affected areas of the value chain. This format allows users to quickly see how SROs are distributed across different sustainability-related topics and where they are concentrated in the business.

<ExampleBox>
**Illustrative SRO Overview Table (for a metals and mining company):**

| Topic | Risk/Opportunity | Time Horizon | Affected Area |
|-------|-----------------|-------------|---------------|
| Climate | **Physical risk:** Increased frequency and severity of weather events may damage critical mine infrastructure and assets, leading to operational downtime and supply chain disruption. | Short, Medium, Long | Owned and operated mine sites; downstream ports and transport corridors |
| Climate | **Transition risk:** Increased carbon regulation across key operating regions may significantly increase operational costs and capital requirements for decarbonisation. | Medium, Long | Upstream energy suppliers; owned and operated mine sites; all jurisdictions |
| Climate | **Transition risk:** Growing demand for low-carbon materials and technologies may reduce the competitiveness of carbon-intensive mining outputs, leading to declining revenues and potential asset stranding. | Short, Medium, Long | Upstream suppliers; owned and operated mine sites |
| Climate | **Opportunity:** Increased demand for key transition materials (lithium, copper, cobalt) driven by the shift towards low-carbon economies, providing an opportunity to reposition as a critical enabler of the energy transition. | Medium, Long | Owned and operated mine sites; downstream customers |
| Nature | **Risk:** Biodiversity-related legal and regulatory pressures may raise costs and threaten the ability to access land, maintain permits, and operate without interruption. | Medium, Long | Upstream suppliers; owned and operated mining sites (particularly mine closure and rehabilitation) |
| Nature | **Risk:** Water scarcity may constrain access to freshwater needed for processing, rehabilitation, and dust suppression, increasing operational disruption and cost. | Short, Medium, Long | Upstream suppliers; owned and operated mining sites |
| Social | **Risk:** Human rights breaches in operations or the supply chain may result in financial losses due to legal claims, fines, project delays, and reputational damage, potentially affecting investor confidence and access to capital. | Medium, Long | Upstream suppliers and contractor activities; owned and operated mine sites |

The time horizons used are: Short term (0-5 years, aligned with the 5-year strategic planning cycle), Medium term (5-15 years, aligned with the 2030 interim emissions target timeframe), Long term (15+ years, aligned with long-term investment and divestment horizons).
</ExampleBox>

This tabular format satisfies the requirements of paragraphs 10 and 13(b) simultaneously: it describes each risk, classifies it by type, assigns a time horizon, and shows where in the value chain the risk is concentrated. It also demonstrates that the company has considered risks beyond climate, which is relevant when governance disclosures describe integrated sustainability oversight.
